Your brokerage app now sells a bitcoin fund next to the rest of your stocks.

No wallet setup. No seed phrase. No list of words to hide.

It feels like the simpler way to get exposure to bitcoin's price.

It is also not the same as holding bitcoin itself. This guide explains the difference in the [SEC's own words](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ETPBulletinSeptember2024), without telling you which one to pick.

## What is a Bitcoin ETF, in plain English?

A fund that trades on a stock exchange and is designed to track the price of bitcoin, without you holding the coins. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ETPBulletinSeptember2024) calls the common version a spot bitcoin exchange-traded product, or ETP. It is a commodity trust that holds bitcoin.

People, and sometimes the funds themselves, call these products ETFs. The SEC says they are not ETFs registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940, even when the name or a headline uses that word.

There is a second type: a futures bitcoin ETP, which holds bitcoin futures contracts instead of bitcoin. The SEC says those are usually structured as ETFs. This page is about the spot version, the one that holds the asset.

If you want the coin itself first, start with [What is Bitcoin?](/articles/what-is-bitcoin/).

## Do you own bitcoin if you own the fund?

No. You own shares of the fund. The [SEC's page on crypto-asset-related investments](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/crypto-asset-related-investments) says that when you buy shares of a fund or ETP with exposure to crypto assets, you are indirectly exposed to the price and volatility of the underlying investments. You do not directly own those investments.

When you buy the crypto asset itself, that same page says you have full ownership, and you access it through a wallet with private keys.

That is the whole fork. Shares, or coins.

## Who holds the keys?

The fund's custodian does, not you. [Spot bitcoin ETPs](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ETPBulletinSeptember2024), the SEC says, are meant to give you exposure to the price without some of the direct risks of transacting on a crypto platform or handling public and private keys yourself. The fund itself still faces those risks.

The [SEC's custody bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) describes the two sides. In self-custody, you have sole control of the private keys, and sole responsibility for them. If the wallet is lost, stolen, damaged, or hacked, you may permanently lose access. In third-party custody, a professional custodian manages and controls access to the keys. If that custodian is hacked, shuts down, or goes bankrupt, you may lose access.

A fund is the second model. You are not holding the [seed phrase](/articles/what-is-a-seed-phrase/). You also cannot restore the coins to a wallet of your own the way a self-custody backup can.

## What does the fund give you that coins do not?

A way to get price exposure inside a brokerage account, without handling keys. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ETPBulletinSeptember2024) lists that as a potential benefit of a spot bitcoin ETP: you can avoid some of the direct risks of personally using a trading platform or a wallet.

You buy and sell shares on a national securities exchange, the same bulletin says, and the product has to register its offering and its securities with the SEC. It also has to publish a prospectus and periodic reports, including the risks that make the investment speculative or risky.

You can read those filings in the SEC's EDGAR system. The bulletin tells you to.

## What do you give up?

The ability to hold, send, or spend the bitcoin itself, and the control that comes with holding the keys. Because you [do not directly own](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/crypto-asset-related-investments) the underlying asset, you cannot send it to someone the way you would from a wallet.

You also pay a sponsor fee that coin holders do not pay. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ETPBulletinSeptember2024) says spot bitcoin ETPs generally pay a fee to the sponsor, and that because the trust does not generate income, that fee typically covers operating expenses. Each time the fee is paid, the amount of the crypto asset represented by your share declines. Even a small fee can have a major impact over time, the bulletin says.

The share price can also differ from the price of bitcoin. The SEC says that can happen because of changing demand for the shares, issues at the issuer, or events in the crypto markets.

And the underlying bitcoin market, the SEC notes, includes trading platforms that are not registered with the SEC, which the bulletin says can raise the potential for fraud and manipulation in that market.

## Why does the "ETF" label matter?

Because it can sound like a mutual fund or a stock ETF, and the legal wrapper is different. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ETPBulletinSeptember2024) says spot bitcoin ETPs are not registered as investment companies under the Investment Company Act of 1940. That means they are not subject to that law's requirements on valuation and custody of fund assets, the way ETFs and mutual funds are.

The [crypto-asset investments page](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/crypto-asset-related-investments) repeats the point. Mutual funds, ETFs, and other funds registered as investment companies have legal requirements intended to protect investors, including rules on valuation, custody, and public disclosure. ETPs register their offerings and make required disclosures, but they are not registered as investment companies, so they are not subject to the same legal requirements.

They are still subject to the antifraud provisions of the federal securities laws. The SEC is not saying the label is a trick. It is saying the word "ETF" in a name or a headline does not automatically bring those extra 1940 Act rules.

## What should you read before you choose either one?

The risk factors, and an honest answer to which problem you are trying to solve. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ETPBulletinSeptember2024) says bitcoin and ether are highly speculative, including when you get exposure through an ETP. It tells you to consider your risk tolerance, to read the prospectus, and to notice that you can lose money.

If your question is "who holds the keys?", the [custody bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) is the cleaner starting point. Self-custody means you hold the [seed phrase](/articles/what-is-a-seed-phrase/). A fund means you do not.
