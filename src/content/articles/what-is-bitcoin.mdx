You download an app because someone said you should "just get some bitcoin."

The screen asks for an amount you cannot picture, of a thing you cannot hold, paid with money you might need for rent.

That pause is a good pause.

[Bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) describes bitcoin as money that uses cryptography, not a central office, to control how new units are created and how they move. The first public version showed up in 2009, under the name Satoshi Nakamoto.

It is one item in a wider bucket. [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/spotlight/crypto-assets) calls that bucket a crypto asset: something generated, issued, or transferred on a blockchain or a similar shared ledger. Coins, tokens, and virtual currencies all sit there. They are not the same product.

This page stays with bitcoin. It will not tell you to buy.

## The app makes it look like a file

People say the bitcoin is "in the phone."

It is not a photo you can save to the camera roll.

The [SEC's custody bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) says a wallet stores private keys. Those are the secret codes that authorize a transfer. The wallet does not store the asset the way a photo app stores a picture.

Two codes get created.

The private key spends. The SEC compares it to a password, then adds the part that matters: once created, it cannot be changed or replaced. Lose it, and you permanently lose access.

The public key receives. It does not reveal the private key, and it cannot spend. The SEC compares it to an email address.

An app can hold the secret for you. Or it can help you hold it. Those are different failures. The buying guide covers the choice when you are ready. [How to buy crypto safely](/articles/how-to-buy-crypto-safely/).

## The original paper is shorter than the pitch

The [original bitcoin paper](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf) describes a payment from one person to another without a financial institution in the middle.

The method is a shared chain of records. Computers do work so the network can agree which payment came first.

You do not need the math.

You need the consequence. There is no teller who can pull the payment back because you tapped the wrong name. [Bitcoin.org's FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says the software is published in the open, and any developer can review it.

## You do not have to buy a whole coin

This is the line that lets people exhale.

The [Bitcoin FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says only 21 million bitcoins will be created. It also says that cap is not a wall against small payments, because a bitcoin breaks into smaller units.

There are 100,000,000 satoshis in one bitcoin.

Eight decimal places. One satoshi is 0.00000001 bitcoin.

Satsplain is named for that unit. "Sats" is the short word. We do not print a dollar price here. Prices move, and a stale number would lie later. The useful fact is smaller than a price. You can transfer a fraction.

## What actually happens after you tap send

"Sent" is not the same word as "settled."

[Bitcoin.org's how-it-works page](https://bitcoin.org/en/how-it-works) is the plain version. A wallet makes an address you can show someone. The page says an address should be used only once. A bit like email, except you do not reuse it.

The blockchain is a shared public ledger. Confirmed payments are written there. That is how a wallet can add up a balance, and how the network checks that the sender is allowed to spend those coins.

A transaction is a transfer between wallets. The private key signs it. The signature is the proof it came from the holder of the key, and it stops other people from editing the payment after it goes out.

Then you wait. Bitcoin.org says a transaction usually gets its first confirmation in about 10 to 60 minutes, through mining.

Mining packs pending payments into a block. The rules make it so changing an older block would break every block after it. Mining also works like a lottery that is hard to keep winning, which makes it hard for one person to rewrite history and reverse their own spending.

So give the payment time. A green check on your screen can be ahead of the ledger.

## There is no headquarters to call

Bitcoin is not a company.

There is no support desk that "runs bitcoin" the way a bank runs a checking account.

The [Bitcoin FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says Satoshi Nakamoto left the project in late 2010 and did not reveal a public identity. The inventor's influence was only the changes other people chose to run. The code is open. Anyone can review it, or publish a modified version.

Open is not the same as unbreakable.

The FAQ says the rules and the cryptography have kept working since 2009. It also says flaws have been found and fixed in the software people use to reach the network. As with other software, safety depends on problems being found and patched.

## Officials do not use one label

A label is not a safety score.

The [CFTC's advisory on virtual currency](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/understand_risks_of_virtual_currency.html) says bitcoin and other virtual currencies have been determined to be commodities under the Commodity Exchange Act. The CFTC says it mainly regulates derivatives such as futures, its watch over the cash market is limited, and it still goes after fraud in that cash market.

The same advisory says virtual currency does not have legal tender status, is not backed by a government or central bank, and takes its value from supply and demand. It also says these prices swing more than traditional currencies.

Taxes use a different word. The [IRS digital assets page](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets) says that for US tax purposes, digital assets are property, not currency. Income is taxable. Selling or exchanging them can create a gain or a loss you have to report. This page is not tax advice. What to write down after a purchase sits on [Taxes and rules](/pillars/taxes-and-rules/).

In April 2026 the SEC posted [Crypto Assets and the Federal Securities Laws](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/crypto-assets-federal-securities-laws), and the page says it was reviewed again in May 2026. Under "Which Crypto Assets Are Not Securities?" bitcoin is listed as an example of a digital commodity. The same page says other crypto assets can still be securities, depending on their features. A line on a government explainer is a place to start reading. It is not a personal answer about a product someone is pitching you.

Insurance gets mixed up with all of the above.

The [FDIC's list of products that are not insured](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/financial-products-not-insured) includes crypto assets. They are not FDIC-insured, even if you bought them at an FDIC-insured bank. Deposit insurance covers deposits. It does not cover a drop in the price.

## The ordinary ways a first try goes wrong

The [CFTC advisory](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/understand_risks_of_virtual_currency.html) names risks of buying on the cash market. Many platforms are not supervised like a bank. Systems may lack the protections you expect. Prices can swing hard. Markets can be manipulated. People will phish or hack. Some platforms trade for themselves while they also serve you.

Then there is the secret.

The [SEC custody bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) says that if you lose the private key, you permanently lose access. Bitcoin does not email a reset link.

And there is the wrong address.

The CFTC guide [Curious About Crypto? Watch Out for Red Flags](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/LearnandProtect/DigitalAssetRedFlags.pdf) says a bitcoin transfer cannot be changed or stopped, typically cannot be recovered, and cannot be disputed like a card payment. There is no chargeback. If a stranger gives you an address, or a square code, you may be sending value to someone you cannot identify.

The 21 million cap does not cancel any of that.

The [Bitcoin FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says new bitcoin is issued more slowly until issuance stops at 21 million, and that miners would then be paid mostly in fees. A capped supply can still meet a collapse in demand. The FAQ says no one is in a position to predict bitcoin's future. Technical failure, competition, and politics remain possible.

We will not guess a price either.

## There is more than one doorway

Buying bitcoin on a crypto app is not the only structure [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/spotlight/crypto-assets) describes. Some people buy the asset. Some buy shares of public companies in related work. Some buy funds or exchange-traded products tied to crypto.

Those are different legal things. Different fees. Different ways to lose money.

This page does not rank them. If you go further, read that SEC page and the documents for the specific product.
