The wallet just put a list of words on the screen.

It says this is the only way to get them back. A checkbox is waiting.

A photo would be faster. A notes app would be easier.

Do not take the photo. This walkthrough shows how to back those words up on paper, how to check the copy, and where it should live. Paper is a complete, free option. The facts come from the [SEC's custody bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0), [bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq), and the [BIP 39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki) backup standard many wallets use.

## What are you backing up, exactly?

The list of words that can restore a self-custody wallet. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) calls this a seed phrase: a random sequence of words that lets you restore the wallet if you lose it, lose its private key, or the hardware or software is damaged.

You may also see it called a recovery phrase or a mnemonic. Same job.

If the words themselves are still new, start with [What is a seed phrase?](/articles/what-is-a-seed-phrase/). This page is the next step: how to make a backup you can actually use.

## Why does a written copy matter?

Because the device is not the backup. [bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/en/you-need-to-know) is blunt. If you permanently lose access to a self-custodied wallet, for example by losing your recovery phrase, the funds are gone permanently. No developer, miner, wallet provider, or exchange can get them back.

The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) says the same thing another way. A private key cannot be changed or replaced. If you lose it, you permanently lose access. The words are how a wallet rebuilds that key.

A working phone is not a plan. A checked paper copy is.

## How do you write the words down safely?

On paper, in order, in a private place, while the wallet still shows them. The [bitcoin.org FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says most modern wallets are backed up with a recovery phrase: writing it down and storing it securely offline lets you restore the entire wallet if the device is lost, stolen, or broken.

Do this:

1. Find a private place. No cameras over your shoulder. No screen sharing.
2. Use paper and a pen that will still be readable later.
3. Write each word in numbered order, exactly as the wallet shows it. [BIP 39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki) treats the words as a sentence. The order is part of the backup.
4. If you set an extra passphrase, write that down too. BIP 39 uses it, together with the words, to make the seed. Leave it blank and the wallet uses an empty one.

Do not invent your own words. BIP 39 is a way to write down randomness the wallet generated. It is not a way to turn a phrase you made up into a wallet seed.

## How do you check that you copied them correctly?

By comparing the paper to the screen, word by word, before you close the wallet. The [BIP 39 English list](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/english.txt) has 2,048 words. If your wallet uses that list and a word on your paper is not on it, you copied it wrong.

Many wallets then ask you to confirm a few of the words. Do that from the paper, not from memory.

The built-in checksum in [BIP 39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki) can catch some mistakes. It can also miss some. The specification says the checksum is short, that some random errors get through, and that it cannot tell you which word to fix. That is why the visual check still matters.

The first four letters of each English BIP 39 word are unique, according to the same specification. If your handwriting turns those letters into something that could be two words, rewrite it.

## Where should the paper live?

In a private, secure place, and not only in one if you can help it. The [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://fcnb.ca/en/news-alerts/cafc-bulletin-cryptocurrency-wallet-fraud) says to keep the recovery phrase in a private and secure place.

[bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/en/secure-your-wallet) says a backup that depends on a single location is a single point of failure. It suggests keeping copies in more than one physical place, using durable storage media.

Two copies in two private places can survive one accident that would have destroyed the only copy. Two copies also mean two places someone else might find. That is the tradeoff. Official guides do not pick the furniture for you. They do say the place should be private, and that one location is a weak plan.

Most modern wallets are deterministic. bitcoin.org says a single backup of the recovery phrase is usually enough to restore past and future addresses, but you should check how your own wallet handles backups.

## Can you keep a photo or a cloud copy?

No. The [bitcoin.org FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says never to store a recovery phrase as a photo or in a cloud service, and never to share it with anyone.

The [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://fcnb.ca/en/news-alerts/cafc-bulletin-cryptocurrency-wallet-fraud) says the same, in a slightly longer list: do not store it in an email, a cloud account, an online document, or a photograph.

A picture in your camera roll is a copy that backups, phones, and cloud accounts can spread without you noticing. An online note is a copy a breached account can leak. The official advice is to keep the words offline.

## What if you set an extra passphrase?

You have to store that too, or the words alone will not open the wallet you meant to open. [BIP 39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki) lets you add a passphrase to the mnemonic. If you do not set one, the method uses an empty string.

Every passphrase produces a valid wallet. Only the correct one produces the wallet that holds your coins. A typo will not always show an error. It can open an empty wallet that looks real.

If you did not set a passphrase, you can ignore this section.

## Is paper enough, or do you need something sturdier?

Paper is enough. It is the free option the official guides describe. The [bitcoin.org FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says writing the recovery phrase down and storing it securely offline is how you back up most modern wallets.

Paper can also be damaged, lost, or found, the same way the [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) says a physical cold-wallet device can be lost, damaged, or stolen. That is why bitcoin.org also says to consider more than one physical place and durable storage media.

A sturdier copy, if you want one, is optional. Nothing in this guide requires you to buy one. A checked paper copy, stored privately, is a complete backup.

## What should you do if someone asks for the words?

Stop, and end the contact. The [bitcoin.org FAQ](https://bitcoin.org/en/faq) says no legitimate person, business, or support team will ever ask for your recovery phrase. The [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://fcnb.ca/en/news-alerts/cafc-bulletin-cryptocurrency-wallet-fraud) says a police service, government agency, financial institution, wallet provider, or legitimate support service will not ask you to send it by email, text, social media, online form, or letter.

The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) is shorter: never share your private keys or seed phrases.

If the message also offers to recover lost coins, treat it as a warning. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre describes that follow-up as recovery fraud. More of the pattern is in [Crypto scams to avoid](/articles/crypto-scams-to-avoid/).
