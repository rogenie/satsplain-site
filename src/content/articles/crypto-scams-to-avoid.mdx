A text lands while you are making coffee.

The person is warm. They do not ask for money. They ask how your day was.

A week later they mention a trading app. The screen shows a profit. You have not withdrawn a dollar.

That script is not rare. [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/spotlight/crypto-assets) describes relationship investment scams: someone hides who they are, reaches out online or by text, earns trust, then steers the target into a fake investment. The [FBI's 2025 crime report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) says the same pattern, at a scale that is hard to picture until you see the number.

## How large are the reported losses?

Cryptocurrency investment fraud was the largest category of reported losses in the FBI's 2025 report, at $7.2 billion. The FBI's report says cryptocurrency investment fraud was the highest source of reported losses to Americans in 2025, at $7.2 billion.

A table in the same [2025 IC3 report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) lists 61,559 complaints and $7.228 billion for that category.

Complaints that involved cryptocurrency at all: 181,565, with $11.366 billion in reported losses. The report says that was up 21 percent in complaints and 22 percent in losses from 2024.

These are people who filed. They are not every victim.

The report also says much of the investment fraud is run by organized groups, including operations in Southeast Asia that have used victims of human trafficking as forced labor. The friendly texter may not be who they claim, and they may not be free to stop.

## How does the friendship scam work?

It starts as a friendly chat and ends with a fake trading app. Here is the sequence the FBI describes.

Contact starts in a text, a social app, an ad, or a dating app. The chat moves to another messaging app. You are folded into a "group" of people who sound like they know markets. They talk about crypto, and sometimes gold.

You send crypto to a platform or an app they name. The screen shows profits. They may offer a loan so you send more.

The profits are a picture. The [FBI report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) says that when people try to withdraw, they are charged taxes and fees. Then the operators disappear with the funds.

## What happens when you try to withdraw?

They ask for another payment before you can take anything out. You ask for your money.

They ask for another payment first. A tax. A verification fee. A release charge.

The [SEC and CFTC alert on fraudulent trading sites](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/watch_out_for_digital_fraud.html) describes this. People are told to pay extra costs, including what the scammers call taxes, before they can take out fake profits. The alert calls that an advance-fee fraud. You pay in order to receive something that is not real.

The [CFTC's fraud page](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/digitalassetfrauds) is blunt about the next mistake. Do not pay more of your own money to "get the profit back." In this kind of platform fraud, the trading did not happen.

If the story needs one more payment before you can leave, you are not in an account. You are in a script.

## What is a recovery scam?

A second stranger offers to get the money back if you pay them first. You search for help. A new account replies. They know the details. They can recover the coins, for a fee.

Stop.

The [FBI report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) says victims are then targeted by recovery scams. The [CFTC infographic](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/LearnandProtect/OCEO_Cryptocurrency_Infographic.pdf) says not to pay a service that claims it can recover lost funds.

The second helper is often the same crime in a new costume.

## What does an unsolicited pitch look like?

Someone you did not ask contacts you and talks as if the money cannot be lost. Some scams skip the friendship and go straight to the promise.

The [SEC and CFTC alert](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/watch_out_for_digital_fraud.html) lists the shape. A return that is described as certain. Language that says you cannot lose. A stranger who contacted you. Pressure to decide immediately. Jargon you are not meant to question. A seller who is not licensed.

We will not repeat their slogans here. The idea is enough. If the safety of the money is the sales line, walk away.

A cousin of that pitch is the sudden tip. The [CFTC's pump-and-dump advisory](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/beware_virtual_currency_pump_dump.html) says not to buy a coin because of one social-media tip or a sudden price jump. Do not join a plan whose point is to drive a price up and leave other people holding it. That conduct can be illegal, and the people who arrive late are the ones who tend to lose.

## When is the payment method the warning?

When they walk you to a crypto ATM, a gift card, a QR code, or a wallet address you did not create. Read this twice if someone is walking you to a store.

The CFTC's August 2026 advisory, [Pause Before You Pay](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/ATM.html), is about unusual ways of sending money. Crypto ATMs. Gift cards. Unfamiliar apps. A QR code. A wire. A courier.

The caller may claim to be your bank, a broker, the government, or tech support. They create fear. They stay on the phone while you pay. They tell you what to say to the clerk. They tell you not to tell your family.

Hang up.

Then contact the real institution with a number you look up, not the number they gave you. The advisory says a transfer to a crypto address you did not create cannot be reversed, no matter the amount.

The [CFTC red-flag guide](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/LearnandProtect/DigitalAssetRedFlags.pdf) says the same thing in four beats. A bitcoin transfer cannot be changed or stopped. It cannot be disputed. It hides who received it. It can be moved on quickly. There is no chargeback.

Another flag in that guide: the "investment" only accepts crypto. They even coach you through buying it somewhere else, then sending it to them.

## How do fake sites and QR codes work?

They send you to a copy of a real site, or they put a code in a message that opens that copy. The site looks perfect. The logo is right. The lock icon is there.

It can still be a copy.

The [CFTC](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/digitalassetfrauds) says not to click links or QR codes in email. If you get an account warning, search for the company yourself and contact support from that site. A look-alike page is a common way to steal the login or the funds.

Do not install an app because a stranger sent the file. Do not give anyone remote control of your computer. The 2026 payment advisory says to avoid unknown software and never to hand over remote access.

## What should you do if you already sent money?

Stop paying, call the bank if a card or wire was used, save the screenshots, and report it. You are not foolish. The script is built to work on careful people.

Do this, in order.

1. Stop sending money. Do not pay the withdrawal fee. Do not pay the recovery fee.
2. If a bank, card, or wire was involved, call the number on the card or the statement. The [CFTC](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/ATM.html) says to use a number you look up, and to talk with someone you trust before an unusual transfer. Speed matters most for payments a bank can still review.
3. Save screenshots, addresses, and the conversation. Write down dates.
4. File with the [FBI IC3](https://complaint.ic3.gov/). Also file with the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).
5. If the pitch was a commodity or a trading scheme, use the [CFTC complaint form](https://www.cftc.gov/complaint). If it was framed as an investment, use [Investor.gov's contact page](https://www.investor.gov/contact-us).
6. If a financial company is involved, you can also send a [CFPB complaint](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/).

The FBI notes that these scams leave people in severe financial and emotional distress. If you are in crisis in the United States, contact the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/988). You can call or text 988.

## What can you do with the next message?

Pause, and do not send money if any line on the card is true. You do not need to win an argument. You need a pause.

The [CFTC's fraud page](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/digitalassetfrauds) fits on a card.

- Check the person with a register, not with their own website.
- Remember that a promise of no loss is a sales line.
- Do not trust a stranger you met online.
- Do not click the link they sent.
- Do not pay extra to unlock your own money.
- Do not keep it a secret from someone who cares about you.

If one line is true, you already have your answer.
