A new wallet app is open. It shows you a list of words and asks you to write them down.

There is a button that says "I saved them." You have not saved them. You are not sure what they are.

Do not tap the button yet. This page explains what those words do, in plain English. It does not tell you to buy anything or which wallet to use. The facts come from the [SEC's custody bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) and the other sources listed below.

## What is a seed phrase?

A seed phrase is a list of words that can restore a crypto wallet. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) calls it a random sequence of words that lets you restore your wallet if you lose it, lose its private key, or the hardware or software is damaged.

You may also see it called a recovery phrase, a backup seed phrase, or a mnemonic phrase. Same thing.

The [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://fcnb.ca/en/news-alerts/cafc-bulletin-cryptocurrency-wallet-fraud) says it works like a master key. Many wallets use 12 or 24 words.

Twelve ordinary words. One very unordinary job.

## What does a crypto wallet actually hold?

Keys, not coins. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) says crypto wallets do not store crypto assets themselves. They store the private keys.

A private key is the secret that authorizes a transfer. The SEC compares it to a password, with one big difference: once created, it cannot be changed or replaced. If you lose it, you permanently lose access to the crypto in that wallet.

The public key is the other half. The SEC compares it to an email address. People can use it to send you crypto. It cannot authorize a transfer out.

The words sit one level above the keys. They are how the wallet rebuilds them.

## How do a few words turn into keys?

A published standard turns random numbers into words, and the words back into the numbers. Most beginners never need the math. The shape helps, though.

The standard is [BIP 39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki), a Bitcoin Improvement Proposal. It starts with a random number. It adds a short checksum, splits the result into chunks, and uses each chunk to pick a word.

The words come from a fixed list. The [English list](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/english.txt) has 2,048 words. BIP 39 allows phrases of 12, 15, 18, 21, or 24 words. The words then produce the seed a wallet uses to make its keys.

Two details matter for you:

- **Order matters.** The words encode a number. Swap two words and you have a different number.
- **The checksum is weak.** BIP 39 itself says the checksum gives only modest odds of catching a mistake and cannot help correct one. Copy carefully.

Some wallets also offer an extra passphrase on top of the words. BIP 39 describes this option. If you set one, it becomes part of the secret, and you need it to restore the wallet too.

## Why is losing the words so serious?

Because there is usually no one to call. With [self-custody](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0), the SEC says you have sole responsibility for keeping track of your private keys and seed phrases. If they are lost or stolen, you may lose access to your crypto.

Phones break. Laptops get stolen. Cold-wallet devices get lost or damaged. The SEC says that last one can mean a permanent loss.

The seed phrase is the backup for all of that.

Lose the device and keep the words: you can usually restore. Lose both: the SEC's word is permanent.

## Why is sharing the words worse than losing them?

Because the person who has them can move the crypto. The [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://fcnb.ca/en/news-alerts/cafc-bulletin-cryptocurrency-wallet-fraud) says anyone who gets the recovery phrase may be able to access the wallet and transfer the cryptocurrency. They may not need your phone, your computer, or your device.

A thief does not need to break into anything. You hand over the words, and they rebuild your wallet on their own machine.

The same bulletin lists how fraudsters try to get the words: fake websites, emails, texts, phone calls, letters, QR codes, and harmful software. They often claim there is a problem with your wallet and that you must act right away.

That pressure is the scam's, not ours. You can take as long as you want.

## Who is allowed to ask for your seed phrase?

Nobody. Not a real support desk, not your bank, not the police, not a regulator.

The [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://fcnb.ca/en/news-alerts/cafc-bulletin-cryptocurrency-wallet-fraud) says a police service, government agency, financial institution, wallet provider, or legitimate support service will not ask you to send it by email, text, social media, online form, or letter.

The [CFTC](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/Beware_Imposters) says it does not audit or verify people's digital wallets and would never ask for private keys or seed phrases. Its [imposter fraud page](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/imposterfrauds) says to verify any call or email before you disclose a seed phrase, end the contact, and find the official website yourself.

"Recovery services" deserve their own line. The [FBI](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2023/PSA230811) warns about fake businesses that contact scam victims, claim they can trace and recover lost crypto, and charge up-front fees. The CFTC says never to pay an up-front fee for fraud recovery.

More patterns are in [Crypto scams to avoid](/articles/crypto-scams-to-avoid/).

## How should you store a seed phrase?

On paper, offline, somewhere private, and never in a photo or the cloud. The [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://fcnb.ca/en/news-alerts/cafc-bulletin-cryptocurrency-wallet-fraud) says to keep the recovery phrase in a private and secure place, and not in an email, cloud account, online document, or photograph.

When a wallet shows you the words:

1. Write the words down, in order, on paper.
2. Check each word against the screen. The [BIP 39 English list](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/english.txt) has 2,048 words. If your wallet uses that list and a word is not on it, you copied it wrong.
3. Store the paper somewhere private and secure.
4. Do not type the words into a site, form, or chat that a message sent you to. The same bulletin says not to click links or scan QR codes in unexpected messages.
5. If anyone asks for them, stop and end the contact.

If you set an extra passphrase, store it too, because [BIP 39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki) uses it to make the seed.

That is the whole routine. Short, and not optional.

## Do you need a seed phrase at all?

Only if you hold your own keys. The [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0) says third-party custodians, including crypto exchanges, manage and control access to your private keys.

So if your crypto sits in an exchange or app account, the company holds the keys. You sign in with a password and a second check instead. The [first-purchase guide](/articles/how-to-buy-crypto-safely/) covers that lock.

The tradeoff runs both ways. The SEC says a custodian that is hacked, shuts down, or goes bankrupt can cost you access. Holding the words yourself means nobody else can lose them for you, and nobody else can restore them.

Neither choice is safe by default. This page does not pick one. The [Wallets and security](/pillars/wallets-and-security/) hub lays out the options side by side.
